How we know, and what this cannot tell you

1. Where the data comes from

Two public NASA records of the atmosphere, month by month:

NameWhat it isYears
AIRSOne instrument on NASA's Aqua satellite. It measures the temperature of the air at 24 heights.2002 to now
MERRA-2A weather model corrected by real measurements. 42 heights. Temperature, water vapor and ozone.1980 to now

We average all the way around the Earth in rings five degrees wide. That keeps the data small and the picture clear, but it hides differences between east and west.

2. How a trend is measured

July is always warmer than January, so we first compare each month only with the same month in other years. Then we look at every possible pair of months and ask how fast the value changed between them. The trend is the middle one of all those answers. One freak month cannot bend it.

3. How we test it

A trend can appear by chance. We ask how steadily the measurements move in one direction, and we allow for the fact that the air "remembers": a warm month is often followed by another warm month. Because we test hundreds of places at once, we also control how many false alarms are allowed among the ones we flag.

4. What the four labels mean

LabelMeaning

Three extra checks can change a label:

  • A second dataset. Where AIRS and MERRA-2 overlap, a change is only "Clear" if both show it.
  • Volcano months. We repeat every trend without the months after big eruptions. If the trend changes by more than half, the label becomes "Disputed".
  • Data-source jumps. MERRA-2 started using new satellites in 1998 and 2004. If the record jumps at those moments, and the jump explains much of the trend, the label becomes "Disputed".

6. What this tool cannot tell you

  • Why. A trend shows what changed, not the cause. Where we mention a cause, we name the study.
  • Your town. Every value is an average around the whole Earth at one distance from the equator.
  • The future. A trend describes the years measured. It does not promise the same pace ahead.
  • Exact heights. Satellites measure by air pressure. Our kilometres are a close conversion, not exact.
  • Water vapor high up. We show water vapor only up to about 8 km. MERRA-2 takes in humidity measurements up to that height. Higher up its water vapor comes from the model, and in the stratosphere it is held close to a fixed yearly pattern, so a trend there would not be a measurement (Davis and others, Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics, 2017).
  • Fine detail. To keep downloads small we read every second grid point of AIRS and every fourth of MERRA-2 before averaging around the Earth. Each band still averages thousands of points a month, but we have not measured how much this thinning changes a trend.
  • Missing spots. Where a satellite could not see (for example below mountain tops, or through thick cloud), the average around the Earth uses only the places it could see. Daytime and night-time passes count equally, whatever their number of measurements.
  • One missing month. MERRA-2 for June 1998 failed to download and is left out. A trend over 46 years does not depend on one month, but it sits next to a data-source change that our jump check examines.
  • The jump check is ours. Its thresholds are our own rule of thumb, not a published standard. It errs toward calling a result "Disputed".
